## Post 1 — Medium length, Personal Brand category

**title:** What "Personal Brand Photography" Actually Means

**slug:** what-personal-brand-photography-actually-means

**category:** Personal Brand

**tags:** personal-brand, entrepreneurs, editorial

**excerpt:** Personal brand photography is one of those phrases that means something different to every photographer who uses it. Here is what it means in my practice, and how I think about the work I do for entrepreneurs, founders, and creative professionals.

**publishedAt:** 2026-05-08

**coverImage:** <TBD>

**body:**

Personal brand photography is one of those phrases that means something different to every photographer who uses it. For some, it means headshots in a coffee shop with a laptop in frame. For others, it means a glossy fashion shoot with the client's logo somewhere off-screen. Both can be valid. Neither is what I usually mean when I use the term.

When I work on a personal brand project, I am thinking about a body of images that can carry someone's public presence for the next twelve to eighteen months. Speaking pages. Press features. Podcast guest cards. The "About" section of a website. The post that goes up the day a book launches. The image that ends up on a slide deck someone else built. These are very different contexts, and yet a personal brand session has to produce work that lives across all of them without looking borrowed from somewhere else.

That is the practical definition. The deeper definition has more to do with alignment than it does with output. Most people I work with arrive having seen their own photos elsewhere — on a website built five years ago, in a corporate headshot taken under fluorescent lights, in iPhone snapshots a friend captured. The images are technically of them. They just do not feel like the person who is now showing up to the session.

> The work, at its best, is matching the outside image to the inside trajectory. Where someone is going, not where they were when their last photos were taken.

In practical terms, this means the session usually starts well before the shoot day. We talk about who the images are for — the kind of audience the person is trying to reach. We talk about wardrobe, but in the context of what story the clothes are doing, not just whether they photograph well. We talk about locations, and whether the person wants to be photographed somewhere that already feels like them, or somewhere they would like to grow into.

On the day, the technical work matters, but it is rarely the part that determines whether the session lands. What determines it is whether the person on the other side of the camera feels prepared, present, and clear about what they want. My job is to set up the conditions where that is possible — and then make sure the images we get are flexible enough to live wherever they need to live for the next year and a half.

A few practical signals that a personal brand session is going well:

- The wardrobe was decided two weeks ago, not the morning of.
- We are shooting in at least two settings, not one, because real public presence rarely happens in one room.
- The deliverable includes both portrait orientations and wide compositions, because slide decks and podcast cards crop differently than Instagram.
- There is at least one image that the client describes as "I do not normally let people photograph me this way, but it feels right."

If you are thinking about a session and not sure whether what you need is personal brand work or something else — portrait, model test, commercial — the simplest filter is the audience question. If the images are for *your* audience, and the goal is for that audience to recognize and trust you, it is probably personal brand work. The rest is a matter of figuring out exactly what alignment looks like for you.

## Post 2 — Long length, Behind the Scenes category

**title:** What I Actually Do Before a Shoot

**slug:** what-i-actually-do-before-a-shoot

**category:** Behind the Scenes

**tags:** process, preparation, behind-the-scenes

**excerpt:** Most of the work on any session happens before the camera comes out. Here is how a typical week leading up to a shoot looks on my end, and what I am thinking about while it is happening.

**publishedAt:** 2026-05-01

**coverImage:** <TBD>

**body:**

Most of the work on any session happens before the camera comes out. Clients sometimes ask what I am doing in the weeks between booking and the actual shoot day — partly because they want to know what is expected of them, and partly because the pace of preparation is different from what they may have experienced with other photographers.

The short answer is that I am doing the work that makes the shoot itself feel calm. The longer answer is below.

### The first conversation

The first thing I want to know after someone books is not what they want to wear or where they want to shoot. It is what the images are for. A session for a website redesign is a different project than a session for a book launch, even if the wardrobe is identical. The end use shapes everything — composition, orientation, the ratio of close portraits to wider environmental shots, whether we need negative space for graphic overlays, and how much variety we should aim for.

This conversation usually happens by email or on a short call within a few days of booking. It does not need to be long, but it needs to be specific. "I want photos for my website" is a starting point; "I want a hero image for my homepage that works at a 16:9 crop, plus six to eight headshots for an Our Team page, plus three or four lifestyle images for blog posts and social" is the actual brief.

### Wardrobe and styling

Wardrobe is the next conversation, and it is usually the one that takes the most time. I do not style clients myself, but I do guide the process closely — what colors will read well against the location backgrounds, what textures hold up under the kind of light we are likely to have, what silhouettes will work for the kind of compositions we are planning.

Most clients I work with bring three to five complete looks to a personal brand session, even if we end up using fewer than that. Bringing options is not about being indecisive; it is about giving us room to respond to how the day actually unfolds. The light at the second location may be cooler than expected. The mood may shift halfway through the morning. Having wardrobe flexibility lets us follow the day instead of fighting it.

### Location scouting

For sessions outside a studio, I usually visit the locations a few days ahead — or at least at the same time of day we will be shooting, the week before. This is the part of preparation clients almost never see, and it is where a surprising amount of the final image quality is decided.

I am looking for things like: where is the light at 9 a.m. versus 11 a.m., what direction is the shadow falling, are there reflective surfaces I can use, where can we stand without picking up unwanted reflections in the background, what does the space sound like (a too-loud cafe makes direction harder), and how the space changes if there is foot traffic.

> Location scouting is not about finding "the perfect spot." It is about knowing the space well enough to make confident decisions in the moment, instead of figuring it out while the client is standing in front of the camera.

### The day before

Twenty-four hours before a shoot, I am doing two things: confirming logistics one last time, and clearing my head.

The logistics part is short — call sheet, arrival times, parking notes, weather check, backup plan if outdoor shoots need to move indoors. I send this to clients in a single message so they can find it without scrolling.

Clearing my head is harder to describe but it matters. I do not look at other photographers' work the night before a shoot. I do not redesign the brief. I trust the preparation I have already done and let the day be the day. Some of the best frames I have ever shot came out of moments I did not plan — and being mentally available to notice them is the actual job.

### What this means for clients

The practical version of all this, from a client's perspective:

- Expect a thoughtful early conversation about end use.
- Expect specific guidance on wardrobe, not vague advice.
- Expect a clear call sheet the day before.
- Do not expect to be asked to "be creative" on the day. That is my job. Your job is to show up, be present, and trust that we have already made most of the hard decisions.

If you have a session coming up — with me or with someone else — and you have not had any of these conversations yet, that is worth raising with your photographer. Preparation that lives only in the photographer's head is preparation that may not survive the shoot day.

## Post 3 — Short length, Pre-Shoot Prep category

**title:** A Note on Wardrobe Timing

**slug:** a-note-on-wardrobe-timing

**category:** Pre-Shoot Prep

**tags:** wardrobe, preparation, practical

**excerpt:** The single most useful thing you can do for a session is decide on wardrobe two weeks before the shoot, not the morning of. Here is why.

**publishedAt:** 2026-04-24

**coverImage:** <TBD>

**body:**

The single most useful thing you can do for a session is decide on wardrobe two weeks before the shoot, not the morning of.

Two weeks gives time for the things that go wrong. A button comes loose and needs replacing. A jacket needs to come back from the cleaner. A blouse you thought worked turns out to read poorly in the light at your kitchen window, which is a reasonable proxy for shoot-day daylight. Online orders arrive late, or arrive in the wrong size, or arrive in a color the photographs differently than it did on screen.

None of these are catastrophes individually. All of them, if discovered the morning of a session, will quietly compress the part of the day that should be spent settling into the shoot. By the time wardrobe is sorted, the calm has already been spent.

A short checklist that has saved a lot of mornings:

- Lay out every full look on a hanger or bed at least one week before, including shoes and any visible accessories.
- Photograph each look on your phone in natural daylight. Look at the photos the next morning, not the same day. You will see things you did not see while standing in front of the mirror.
- Wash and iron everything five to seven days out. Day-of ironing rarely produces the result you wanted.
- Bring at least one extra option per look, even if you are sure of the choice. The session will reveal what works.

The goal is for wardrobe to be the part of the day you do not have to think about. Everything else — light, location, energy, direction — benefits from that.
